Spring Oscillators

Using Derivatives to Model Motion

A simple numerical simulation built from a differential equation.

What Is An Oscillator?

  • A mass attached to a spring moves back and forth.
  • The farther it stretches, the stronger the restoring force.
  • This creates repeating motion called oscillation.
  • Many real systems behave this way.

Derivatives Describe Change

Position changes according to velocity:

dx/dt = v

Velocity changes according to acceleration:

dv/dt = a

We will approximate these derivatives with small numerical steps.

Hooke's Law

F = -kx   ⇒   a = -(k/m)x
  • k is the spring constant.
  • Higher k means a stiffer spring.
  • The negative sign pulls the spring toward equilibrium.
  • We set m = 1, so a = -kx.

Semi-implicit (Symplectic) Euler

velocity = velocity + acceleration * dt
position = position + velocity * dt   // uses the NEW velocity

Repeating these updates produces a simulation of the differential equation.

Plain (explicit) Euler would use the old velocity for the position update. For a spring, that version gains energy every step and the oscillation grows.

Order matters. Updating v first and then x with the new v is symplectic Euler, which keeps energy bounded. Swap the two lines and the amplitude grows. Good moment to have students try it.

What Should We Observe?

  • Small k produces slow oscillations.
  • Large k produces rapid oscillations.
  • Stronger restoring forces change velocity more quickly.
  • The graph shows how the differential equation behaves.

Why This Matters

  • Physics simulations
  • Engineering models
  • Biological systems
  • Economics and feedback systems
  • Computer animation and games

Takeaway

A differential equation can become a working simulation through small numerical updates.
